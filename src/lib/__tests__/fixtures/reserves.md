2.4.1 Estimate Costs

163

Cost estimating produces a quantitative appraisal of the money a project is likely to need. Teams refine the appraisal as more becomes known, and the estimate carries a stated range of uncertainty rather than a single number. Estimates should name their basis: the assumptions, the constraints, and the method used to arrive at them.

2.4.2 Set Aside Reserves

A contingency reserve is money held for risks the team has already identified and analysed. It belongs to the project manager, sits inside the cost baseline, and is released when a known risk occurs or retired when the risk passes. Spending it does not require a change request because it was planned for exactly this purpose.

<!-- page 164 -->
164

A management reserve covers unknown unknowns: work that nobody could have foreseen at planning time. It is controlled by management, sits outside the cost baseline, and drawing on it moves money into the baseline, which is why a change request is required. Neither reserve funds a change in scope; scope changes go through change control and are funded separately.

2.4.3 Track Spend Against the Baseline

Tracking compares planned value, earned value and actual cost at regular intervals. A cost performance index below one signals overspend relative to work completed, and a schedule performance index below one signals that work is behind plan. The manager analyses the variance before proposing any corrective action, because a single reading may be noise.

165

Forecasting extends the trend to completion. Estimate at completion can be computed in several ways depending on whether current variances are expected to persist. The choice of formula is itself an analysis step and should be recorded with its rationale.
